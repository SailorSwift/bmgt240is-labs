STOP 1 · THE BUSINESS QUESTION

Were Bridger's pricing assumptions right?

Bridger Health Insurance sells individual health plans. This year's prices were built on two assumptions from the pricing team:

Average annual cost per customer$12,000
Share of customers who smoke15%

If either is off, every plan is priced wrong. An analyst pulls a random sample of 50 customers from insurance.csv to check.

Bridger and both assumptions are hypothetical, chosen for teaching. The customers are real rows of the course's insurance data.

The five steps, same as 9.3
  1. State H0 and Ha from the business question.
  2. Choose α (we use .05).
  3. Compute the test statistic from the sample.
  4. Find the p-value.
  5. Decide, then say what it means for the business.

What changes today is only step 3: how we measure the spread.

STOP 2 · 9.4 · POPULATION MEAN, σ UNKNOWN · TWO-TAILED

Is the average charge different from $12,000?

Workbook sheet: 9.4 Charges (50).

Hypotheses

H0: μ = 12,000
Ha: μ ≠ 12,000

Two-tailed: Bridger needs to know if the assumption is off in either direction. Too high or too low, the prices are wrong.

The 50 customers
n50
Sample mean x$11,935.39
Median$7,733.60
Sample std. deviation s$11,707.09

The mean is far above the median: a few very expensive customers pull it up. Charges are right-skewed. With n = 50, the Central Limit Theorem still lets us use t, but keep this in mind.

No σ: use s, and use t

Nobody knows the standard deviation of all customers' charges, so we estimate it with the sample's s. That extra uncertainty is why we use the t distribution with n − 1 = 49 degrees of freedom instead of z.

Predict first

The sample mean is only $65 below $12,000. Will we reject H0?

STOP 3 · 9.5 · POPULATION PROPORTION · UPPER TAIL

Do more than 15% of customers smoke?

Workbook sheet: 9.5 Smokers (50). Same 50 customers.

Hypotheses

H0: p ≤ .15
Ha: p > .15

Upper tail: the worry is more smokers than the surcharge was priced for.

The data is yes / no, so we count
n50
Smokers =COUNTIF(A2:A51,"yes")7
Sample proportion p = 7 / 500.14
Can we use the normal curve?

Need n·p0 ≥ 5 and n(1 − p0) ≥ 5: 50 × .15 = 7.5 and 50 × .85 = 42.5. Both pass.

Why this one is z, not t

H0 hands us the spread. If p really is .15, the standard error is exactly

σp = √( p0(1 − p0) / n ) = √(.15 × .85 / 50) = 0.0505

Nothing is estimated, so there is no extra uncertainty: z. Note it uses p0, not p.

Predict first

p = 0.14 is below .15, in a test about more than .15. Will the p-value be small or large?

StepExcelResult
z = (p − p0) / σp=(D10-D11)/D16−0.198
p-value, upper tail=1-NORM.S.DIST(z,TRUE)0.578
Critical value z.05=NORM.S.INV(1-0.05)1.645

p = 0.578 > .05 · Do not reject H0

When p lands on the wrong side of p0 for an upper-tail test, z is negative and the p-value is above .5. The sample points the other way, so it cannot be evidence for Ha.

Both flip. With all 1,338 customers, the average cost is about $13,270, not $12,000, and about 20.5% smoke, not 15%. Both assumptions were wrong. The sample of 50 just could not see it.

“Do not reject” never meant “the assumption is right.” It meant the 50-customer sample could not tell. Missing a real difference like this is a Type II error (9.2). This sample also happened to include only 7 smokers, fewer than typical: that is ordinary sampling luck.

The gap never changes. Only the standard error shrinks, as s/√n. Any gap, however tiny, becomes “significant” once n is large enough. That is the textbook's warning in 9.6, and its Table 9.5 shows the same thing for 84.5 vs 84 seconds.

STOP 6 · 9.6 · STATISTICAL VS. PRACTICAL SIGNIFICANCE

Significant is not the same as important

Workbook sheet: 9.6 Compare. A p-value says whether a difference is real. Only the business can say whether it matters.

Case 1 · Charges: significant, and it matters
Gap per customer$1,270
× 1,338 customers≈ $1.7 million

Bridger is under-pricing by about $1.7 million a year on these customers. That is worth acting on.

Case 2 · BMI: significant, but does it matter?
Average BMI, all 1,33830.66
Cutoff being tested30
p-value (upper tail)0.00004

Discuss: the difference is almost certainly real. Is 0.66 BMI points big enough to change any pricing decision?

Rule for your project: before you run a test, decide how big a difference would change the decision. Then report both: is it significant, and is it big enough?

9.6 in two sentences

Big samples find small differences: with thousands of rows, almost every test rejects. So a large-sample result needs a second question: is the difference big enough to matter?
