STOP 1 · THE BUSINESS QUESTION

Each year of age adds about $258. Is that real, or sampling noise?

Last time Bridger's line was y = 3,165.89 + 257.72x, with r² = 0.089. Before anyone builds age-based prices on it, the pricing team asks: if age had no relationship with charges at all, could a sample like ours still produce a slope of $258?

Same five-step test as Ch 9, new parameter

H0: β1 = 0 (age is not related to charges)
Ha: β1 ≠ 0

β1 is the slope for all of Bridger's customers. b1 = 257.72 is our estimate of it.

Predict first

The test only works if the error term ε behaves. So first, §14.4: what are we assuming about ε, and does Bridger's data go along?

STOP 2 · 14.4 · ASSUMPTIONS ABOUT THE ERROR TERM ε

Check the model before you trust the test

Workbook sheet: 14.4 Assumptions. Each residual y − y is our stand-in for one customer's ε.

Why these four assumptions?

Stops 3–5 judge the slope by asking how many standard errors b1 sits from 0. Every standard error, p-value and interval in 14.5 is built from one number: s, the typical miss of the line. The four assumptions are the conditions that make s a fair ruler. Think of a bathroom scale: it has to be zeroed, steady, and read the same way every time, or the number it shows means nothing. Excel never checks them. It prints a p-value either way.

1 · E(ε) = 0 at every x

Why we need it: the line has to be the true average y at each x. Otherwise b1 describes the wrong slope.

If it fails: predictions are too high in some ranges and too low in others, as in the curve example below. The slope itself is misleading.

2 · Same spread at every x

Why we need it: we use one s for every customer. If misses are tiny at some ages and huge at others, one number can't describe both.

If it fails: standard errors and intervals are wrong: too narrow where the data are noisy, too wide where they're tight.

3 · Independent errors

Why we need it: every row must bring new information. The standard error shrinks as n grows only because each row counts as a fresh customer.

If it fails (duplicate rows, monthly data that move together): the real n is smaller than it looks, the SE comes out too small, and results look significant when they aren't.

4 · ε is normal

Why we need it: it lets us read p-values from the t distribution, which matters most with small samples.

If it fails: with a small n the p-values can't be trusted. With a big n like Bridger's 1,338, the slope test still works (Central Limit Theorem), but a “± range” for one customer is wrong, because misses aren't symmetric.

Assumption 1 up close · E(ε) = 0 at every x

Least squares always makes the residuals average exactly $0 overall. Does that prove assumption 1?

No. The overall $0 is automatic: least squares guarantees it for any data, even data that curves. Assumption 1 is about each x, so check it one slice at a time.

Why always $0? Reason 1, the intuition. Suppose the errors averaged +$500: customers sit $500 above the line on average. Then sliding the whole line up $500 would bring it closer to the data and make the sum of squared errors smaller. Least squares picks the line with the smallest sum of squared errors, so it has already made that move. It only stops when the errors average exactly $0. Try it:

Slide the line$0
Average error$0
Sum of squared errors178.54 bn
Extra error vs least squares+0.0 million

At $0 the line is the least-squares line: the smallest possible sum of squared errors.

Reason 2, the formula. The intercept is b0 = y − b1x, so the line always passes through the point of averages (x, y). For Bridger: 13,270.42 − 257.72 × 39.207 = 3,165.89, the intercept. Add up every error: Σ(y − b0 − b1x) = ny − nb0 − b1nx = n(y − b0 − b1x) = n × 0 = 0.

The line is meant to be the average charge at each age. ε is “actual − that average.”

If 40-year-olds' errors averaged +$3,000, the true average at 40 would be $3,000 above the line, and the line would be wrong at that age. So assumption 1 really says: a straight line is the right shape for the averages. Misses are random, not predictable.

How to spot it in your own project: run Excel's Regression tool with Residual Plots ticked. If the residuals form a flat band around 0, like Bridger's ages, the straight line is fine. If they form a frown (this example) or a smile, the relationship curves. Say so in your report, and don't use the line to predict outside your data.

Assumption 2 up close · the same spread at every x

Bridger's typical miss is s = $11,560. When is it fair to use that one number for every customer, young and old?

Only if misses are about as big at every age. Every standard error, p-value and interval uses one s for everyone. If young customers' misses were tiny and older customers' were huge, one number would be too big for some and too small for others.

Picture a band of misses around the line. Assumption 2 says the band is the same width everywhere, not a funnel.

Same width: one ruler, s, measures every customer fairly. A funnel: the ruler is too long at the narrow end and too short at the wide end.

When it fails · a funnel

Hypothetical data, made up for teaching. 120 customers of a store: yearly income and yearly spending there, both in $ thousands.

The story. Customers with low incomes all spend about the same small amount. Customers with high incomes vary a lot: some spend big, some barely shop.

Look at the dots: tight on the left, spread out on the right. The line can still go through the middle, but the misses grow with income.

The funnel. This view shows each customer's miss (actual minus the line). Each bar is ±1 SD of the misses for one income group.

The bars get taller from left to right: the misses fan out like a funnel. Compare Bridger's bars on the left, which stay the same height.

One ruler for all. Regression uses one s for everyone. The shaded band is ±2s around the line. If assumption 2 held, it would catch about 95% of customers in every income group.

Read the percentages along the top: almost everyone is inside on the left, but the right side spills out. Red dots are customers outside the band.

Why it matters. Standard errors, p-values and intervals all use one s, so every customer gets the same shaded band: ±6.5. The dashed boxes show the band each income group actually needs, ±2 × that group's own SD.

Lowest incomes: they only need ±2.7, so the shaded band is 2.4× too wide. Predictions for them look much less certain than they really are. Highest incomes: they need ±8.5, so the band is 24% too narrow and catches only 86% of them instead of 95%. Predictions look more certain than they are, for exactly the customers who spend the most.

Assumption 3 up close · independent errors

Copy all 1,338 rows and paste them under the data, so there are 2,676 rows. Did you learn anything new about Bridger's customers?

No new information. But Excel doesn't know that. It treats every row as a new, separate customer, so the standard error shrinks as if you had twice the customers. The results look more certain than they are. Assumption 3 says every row is its own, independent piece of information.

Independent means one customer's miss tells you nothing about the next one's.

It breaks when rows are linked: duplicate rows, the same customer counted twice, or data over time, where a good month tends to follow a good month. You check it mostly by asking how the data were collected, not with a formula.

Hypothetical data, made up for teaching. One store's sales for 36 months in a row, in $ thousands.

The story. Sales grow over three years, but they also move in streaks: a good month is usually followed by another good month, and a slow stretch lasts a while.

Each month is linked to the month before. That's the opposite of independent.

The trend line. Regression on month number gives a straight upward trend. So far nothing looks wrong.

The line sits near the middle of the data, and the misses average 0, as always.

Misses in time order. Each bar is one month's miss: gold above the line, red below.

Why it matters. Because each month mostly repeats the one before, 36 linked months carry the information of far fewer independent ones. Excel still counts 36.

So the standard error comes out too small, and the trend looks more certain than it is, just like the copied rows on the left. With data over time, always plot the misses in time order and look for long runs.

Assumption 4 up close · the misses follow a bell curve

If the misses followed a bell curve, about 31 of 1,338 customers would sit more than 2s ($23,000) above the line, and about 31 that far below it. What does Bridger have?

Lopsided: 147 above, 0 below. A bell curve is balanced: small misses are common, big ones rare, and the same on both sides. Bridger's misses pile up around −$6,000, and a long tail of smokers stretches far above.

Normal means the misses form a bell: most small, a few big, balanced on both sides.

The t test reads its p-value from a bell-shaped curve, so with small samples a lopsided shape makes the p-value unreliable. With a big sample the slope test survives, but a “± range” for one customer doesn't.

Bridger · the shape of the misses

Bars count customers by the size of their miss, in $2,500 steps. The curve shows what a bell with the same spread would look like.

The bars don't match the bell: a tall pile around −$6,000 (most non-smokers cost about $6,000 less than the line says) and a long right tail (smokers). Turn on “Color smokers” to see where the tail comes from. Assumption 4 is violated.

Why it matters · a “± range” for one customer

A common shortcut: “a customer's charge is the line ± 2s.” With a bell curve, that range catches 95% of customers and misses the rest evenly on both sides.

inside “line ± 2s”outside it (below or above)

Bridger's range catches fewer customers than promised, and every miss is on the same side: smokers who cost far more than the range allows. Pricing on “line ± 2s” would underprice exactly those customers.

Why the slope test still works: with 1,338 customers, the sampling distribution of b1 is close to a bell anyway (Central Limit Theorem, Ch 7). With 5 customers, as in Stop 3, we couldn't lean on that.

Section 14.5 · Stops 3–7

Testing for Significance: is the slope real, or just sampling noise?

Stop 2 checked that the model is fit to test. Now we test it. If β1 = 0, x tells us nothing about y. 14.5 gives two tests of that idea, the t test and the F test, plus a confidence interval for β1 that says how big the slope is.

Why test the slope at all?
  1. Excel always gives you a slope, even when there's nothing there. In Project 1, income and investments had no real link, and Excel still reported a slope (0.00137). A line always exists. The question is whether it means anything.
  2. Our slope comes from a sample. A different group of 1,338 customers would give a slightly different slope. Even if age truly didn't matter, chance alone would push the slope a bit above or below 0. The test asks: is $258 bigger than chance could produce?
  3. Real money rides on the answer. Bridger should price by age only if the age effect is real. Pricing on noise overcharges some customers and undercharges others.
  4. It tells you how big, not just whether. The confidence interval turns “age matters” into a planning range: about $214 to $302 more per year of age.
  5. Your final project needs it. For your regression, the report has to say whether the relationship is significant, and how big it is.
See it · what chance alone looks like

Shuffle Bridger's charges so each customer gets someone else's charge at random. That breaks any real link to age. Then fit the line again. Every shuffle gives the slope that chance alone would produce.

Click Shuffle 200 times. Where do chance slopes land, compared with the real $258?

Why that matters · click a reason

This picture is the p-value. The p-value answers: “If age had nothing to do with charges, how often would chance give a slope as big as ours?” Count the shuffles that reached $258: none. So the p-value is tiny (Excel says 4.9 × 10⁻²⁹). The t test gets the same answer with a formula instead of thousands of shuffles.

Compare a slope that isn't real. If you ran the same shuffles on Project 1's income vs investments, the real slope (0.00137) would land right in the middle of the chance pile. That's what p = 0.95 means: chance makes slopes like that all the time. Same Excel output format, opposite meaning. Only the test tells them apart.

For Bridger, it changes the decision. Because $258 is far outside what chance produces, Bridger can treat “older customers cost more” as real and build it into prices. If $258 had sat inside the pile, raising prices by age would mean charging people for noise.

For your project: find your own slope's p-value in Excel's output (the x row, “P-value”). Below 0.05: your slope is outside the chance pile, so say “significant” and give the confidence interval. Above 0.05: it sits inside the pile, so say the data can't show a relationship, and don't build recommendations on that slope.

Key terms · Section 14.5
Test for significance H₀: β₁ = 0 · Hₐ: β₁ ≠ 0
Asks whether x and y are related in the population. If β₁ = 0, the mean of y doesn't change with x. Rejecting H₀ means there is a significant relationship.
Mean square error MSE = s² = SSE/(n − 2)
The estimate of σ², the variance of the error term ε. Here: 133,640,741.
Standard error of the estimate s = √MSE
The typical size of a miss, the distance between a point and the line. Here: $11,560.
Degrees of freedom n − 2
Two are lost because two values, b₀ and b₁, were estimated from the data. Here: 1,338 − 2 = 1,336.
Sampling distribution of b₁
The slope would change from sample to sample. b₁ is one draw from that distribution, which is centered on β₁.
Estimated standard deviation of b₁ sb₁ = s / √Σ(x − x)²
How much b₁ typically varies from sample to sample. Excel calls it the slope's “Standard Error.” Here: 22.50.
t test statistic t = b₁ / sb₁
How many standard errors b₁ sits from 0. Uses the t distribution with n − 2 df. Here: 257.72 / 22.50 = 11.45.
Rejection rule
p-value approach: reject H₀ if p-value ≤ α. Critical value approach: reject H₀ if t ≤ −tα/2 or t ≥ tα/2.
Confidence interval for β₁ b₁ ± tα/2 sb₁
A range of plausible values for the true slope. If it doesn't contain 0, reject H₀ at that α. Here (95%): $213.58 to $301.87 per year of age.
Mean square due to regression MSR = SSR / 1
SSR divided by the number of independent variables, which is 1 in simple regression.
F test statistic F = MSR / MSE
Compares explained to unexplained variation, with 1 and n − 2 df. In simple regression F = t², and the two tests give the same p-value. Here: F = 131.17 = 11.45².
ANOVA table
The table that organizes the F test: sums of squares (SSR, SSE, SST), degrees of freedom, mean squares (MSR, MSE), F, and its p-value. Excel labels that p-value “Significance F.”
Statistical vs practical significance
A significant result says β₁ is probably not 0. It doesn't say the effect is large or useful. The confidence interval shows how big it is.
Cautions about significance
Rejecting H₀ doesn't prove cause and effect. It doesn't prove the relationship is truly linear. And it holds only within the range of x in the data (here, ages 18–64).
