STOP 1 · THE BOOK'S EXAMPLE · HILLTOP COFFEE

Is Hilltop Coffee underfilling its cans?

Every can of Hilltop Coffee says it holds 3 pounds. The Federal Trade Commission (FTC) checks claims like this.

No filling machine is perfect: some cans come out a little heavy, some a little light. The FTC does not care about one can. It cares about the average weight of all the cans Hilltop fills, which is the population mean μ.

The FTC can't weigh every can, so it weighs a sample of 36.

The question: the sample averages 2.92 pounds, which is below 3. Is that just luck in which 36 cans were picked, or is it evidence that the average of all cans is below 3?

What we know
Label claim3 lb
Sample size, n36 cans
Population std. dev., σ (from past tests)0.18 lb
Shape of fill weightsnormal
Sample mean, x2.92 lb
We answer it in four moves
  1. State the hypotheses H0 and Ha (Stop 2).
  2. Choose the level of significance α (Stop 3).
  3. Measure the evidence: the test statistic z and the p-value (Stop 4).
  4. Decide: reject H0 if the p-value ≤ α (Stop 4).

In practice, moves 1 and 2 are decided before anyone weighs a can.

STOP 2 · STATE THE HYPOTHESES

H0: Hilltop is honest until the sample says otherwise

The hypotheses, in symbols and in words

H0: μ ≥ 3

On average, Hilltop's cans hold at least 3 pounds. The label is honest.

Ha: μ < 3

On average, the cans hold less than 3 pounds. Hilltop is underfilling.

Hilltop gets the benefit of the doubt, just like a defendant in court. We assume H0 is true and reject it only if the sample gives strong evidence against it. Ha is what the FTC would have to show before it takes action.

Three rules for writing H0
  1. H0 is about μ, never about x̄. We already know x̄ = 2.92, so there's nothing to test about it. The unknown is the mean of all cans.
  2. H0 always holds the equal sign: =, ≥, or ≤. Ha gets the strict sign: <, >, or ≠.
  3. Ha is the claim that needs evidence. The FTC needs evidence of underfilling, so μ < 3 goes in Ha.
If we reject H0The FTC has evidence that the cans average less than 3 lb and can act against Hilltop.
If we do not reject H0There isn't enough evidence of underfilling. This does not prove the cans are full. The sample just couldn't show otherwise.

STOP 3 · CHOOSE THE LEVEL OF SIGNIFICANCE

α: how much risk of a false accusation will we accept?

α = 0.01 in plain English

If Hilltop is filling its cans correctly, there's at most a 1% chance that our test wrongly accuses them of underfilling.

Imagine the FTC running this test 100 times, each time with 36 new cans from an honest Hilltop. Just by bad luck, a few samples will come out light. With α = 0.01, at most about 1 of those 100 tests would wrongly conclude “underfilling.”

Rejecting H0 when it is actually true is a Type I error. α is the probability of a Type I error that we are willing to accept. It is figured at μ = 3, the edge of H0. If the cans actually average more than 3 pounds, the risk is even smaller.

Who picks α, and when?

The decision maker picks α before looking at the data. The FTC director chose 0.01 because falsely accusing a company is serious and could mean fines or a lawsuit against a firm that did nothing wrong.

α = 0.10willing to be wrong 1 time in 10
α = 0.051 time in 20 (the most common choice)
α = 0.01 · Hilltop1 time in 100

A smaller α makes it harder to reject H0, so it takes stronger evidence to accuse Hilltop.

STOP 4 · MEASURE THE EVIDENCE AND DECIDE

The p-value: how surprising is 2.92 if Hilltop is honest?

Step A · How far below 3 is 2.92?

The raw gap (0.08 lb) means little by itself. We measure it in standard errors, the typical amount a 36-can average moves around just by chance.

σ/√n = 0.18/√36 = 0.18/6 = 0.03 lb

z =2.92 − 30.03= −2.67

The sample mean sits 2.67 standard errors below 3. This z is the test statistic.

Step B · The p-value in plain English

The p-value answers one question: if Hilltop's cans really average 3 pounds, what is the chance that 36 randomly chosen cans would average 2.92 pounds or less?

p-value = P(z ≤ −2.67) = 0.0038

That is about 4 samples in every 1,000. But we actually got such a sample. So there are only two explanations:

Explanation 1Hilltop is honest, and the FTC hit a 4-in-1,000 stroke of bad luck.
Explanation 2Hilltop is not honest: the cans really average less than 3 pounds.

Explanation 2 is far easier to believe, so the sample is strong evidence against H0. Explanation 1 is still possible, though. If it is the truth, rejecting H0 is a Type I error, and α caps how often that happens.

What the p-value is not: it is not the chance that Hilltop is honest. It starts by assuming Hilltop is honest and then asks how unusual our sample would be.

Step C · Decide: compare the p-value with α

0.0038 ≤ 0.01 → reject H0

The sample is rarer than the 1-in-100 line the director drew. In words: the FTC has strong evidence that Hilltop's cans average less than 3 pounds.

Sample mean x 2.920
z = (x̄ − 3) / 0.03−2.67
p-value = P(z ≤ test statistic)0.0038
Compare with α = 0.010.0038 ≤ 0.01

Reject H₀

The shaded area is the p-value. Start at the book's sample, x̄ = 2.92, then drag x̄ up toward 3: the shaded area grows, the sample looks less surprising, and the evidence weakens.

STOP 5 · NAME WHAT YOU JUST DID

The key terms behind the coffee test

Each term below is a step you just took with Hilltop.

Test statistic

A statistic whose value helps determine whether H0 should be rejected.

For a mean with σ known, it counts how many standard errors x̄ is from μ0:

z =x − μ0σ / √n

Hilltop: z = −2.67.

Textbook Ch 9 key terms; equation (9.1).

p-value

A probability that measures the evidence against H0. Smaller p-values mean more evidence against H0.

It is the chance of a test statistic at least as extreme as ours if H0 were true. Rule: reject H0 if p-value ≤ α. Hilltop: 0.0038.

Textbook Ch 9 key terms; Section 9.3.

Critical value

A value that is compared with the test statistic to determine whether H0 should be rejected.

It cuts off an area of α in the tail. If z falls beyond it, reject H0. Hilltop: −2.33 (Stop 6).

Textbook Ch 9 key terms.

One-tailed and two-tailed tests

One-tailed test: H0 is rejected for values of the test statistic in one tail of its sampling distribution (Ha uses < or >). Hilltop is a lower tail test.

Two-tailed test: H0 is rejected for values in either tail (Ha uses ≠). See Stop 7.

Textbook Ch 9 key terms.

The idea in one sentence

Assume H0 is true. If our sample would be very unusual under that assumption (small p-value), we stop believing H0.

Like the jury: if the evidence would be very unlikely for an innocent person, the jury rejects “innocent.”

STOP 6 · TWO APPROACHES, ONE DECISION

p-value approach vs. critical value approach

p-value approach

Find the tail area beyond our z, then compare it with α.

p = 0.0038 ≤ α = 0.01
→ reject H0

Advantage: the p-value tells you how significant the result is. It is also called the observed level of significance: H0 would be rejected for any α ≥ 0.0038.

Critical value approach

Find the z that cuts off α in the tail, then compare our z with it.

−z.01 = −2.33
z = −2.67 ≤ −2.33 → reject H0

Rejection rule for a lower tail test: reject H0 if z ≤ −zα.

Level of significance

The two approaches always agree: whenever the p-value ≤ α, z is beyond the critical value. The shaded α region and the test statistic tell the same story. Uses the x̄ from Stop 4.

STOP 7 · THE BOOK'S EXAMPLE · TWO-TAILED

MaxFlight golf balls: too short or too long is a problem

MaxFlight makes golf balls. Its production process is set so that the balls fly an average of 295 yards off the tee. Both directions are a problem:

Too short (μ < 295)Golfers buy a ball that goes farther. MaxFlight loses sales.
Too long (μ > 295)The balls break the USGA distance standard and can't be used in official play.

So the quality team regularly checks the process by testing a sample of n = 50 balls. Past data give σ = 12 yards.

What we know
Target mean295 yd
Sample size, n50 balls
Population std. dev., σ12 yd
Level of significance, α0.05
Sample mean, x297.6 yd

The question: 297.6 is 2.6 yards above the target. Is that just the luck of which 50 balls were picked, or is the process off target?

Step 1 · State the hypotheses

H0: μ = 295

The process is on target: the balls average 295 yards. Keep it running.

Ha: μ ≠ 295

The process is off target, either too short or too long. Stop it and adjust.

H0 holds the equal sign, and we assume the process is fine until the sample shows otherwise. Ha uses ≠ because either direction is a problem. That is what makes this a two-tailed test.

Step 2 · Level of significance in plain English

Even when the process works perfectly, 50 random balls won't average exactly 295 yards. Luck pushes the sample average a little up or down. Once in a while, luck alone pushes it far enough that the test says “off target” even though nothing is wrong. That mistake is a Type I error.

α = 0.05 means: if the process really is on target, the test will wrongly say “off target” 5% of the time, about 1 check in every 20.

Each false alarm means MaxFlight shuts down and adjusts a process that was fine. α is the false-alarm rate the quality team agrees to live with.

Step 3 · How far from 295 is 297.6?

σ/√n = 12/√50 = 1.7 yards (one standard error)

z =297.6 − 2951.7= 1.53

The sample mean sits 1.53 standard errors above the target.

Step 4 · The p-value in plain English

If the process really averages 295 yards, what's the chance that 50 balls would average at least 2.6 yards away from 295, in either direction?

Why “either direction”? A sample at 292.4 (2.6 yards short) would be just as much evidence that the process is off target as our 297.6. Ha doesn't care which way, so the p-value counts both tails:

upper tail: P(z ≥ 1.53) = 0.0630
lower tail: P(z ≤ −1.53) = 0.0630
p-value = 2 × 0.0630 = 0.1260

That is about 1 sample in 8. A gap like ours happens all the time from luck alone when the process is on target, so it is not surprising.

Step 5 · Decide

0.1260 > 0.05 → do not reject H0

In words: the sample does not give enough evidence that the process is off target, so MaxFlight keeps it running.

This does not prove the mean is exactly 295. It only means a 2.6-yard gap is too easy to get by chance to justify stopping the line.

Same decision with critical values

A two-tailed test splits α in half: 0.025 in each tail, so the critical values are ±z.025 = ±1.96. Reject H0 if z ≤ −1.96 or z ≥ 1.96. Our z = 1.53 is between them, so do not reject.

Hilltop vs. MaxFlight
Hilltop · lower tailOnly underfilling matters (Ha: μ < 3). The p-value is one tail, to the left of z. Critical value −2.33 at α = 0.01.
MaxFlight · two-tailedToo short or too long matters (Ha: μ ≠ 295). The p-value is both tails: 2 × the tail beyond |z|. Critical values ±1.96 at α = 0.05.
