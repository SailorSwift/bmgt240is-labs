Bridger Health Insurance wants age-based prices. That is question Q3 in our class-example proposal: does a customer's age predict their annual medical charges?

In Ch 9 and 10 we tested one number (a mean, a proportion, a difference). Regression asks a different question: how does y change when x changes?

14.1 · The idea in one picture

Schematic, not data. Every customer is a dot. The dashed line is the truth for all of Bridger's customers, which nobody knows. The solid line is our best guess from the data. The red gap is how far one customer sits from our line.

14.1 · Three equations, one idea: read each one
① Regression model

y = β0 + β1x + ε

Says: one customer's charges = the population line at their age + that customer's own error ε.

For Bridger: a customer's charges = (what a typical customer of that age costs) + (everything else about them: smoking, health, luck).

β0, β1, ε unknown

② Regression equation

E(y) = β0 + β1x

Says: the average y at each x. Take the expected value of ①: the errors average out to 0, so ε drops out.

For Bridger: E(y) is the mean charges of all customers of a given age. It is the dashed line in the picture.

a population line: unknown

③ Estimated regression equation

y = b0 + b1x

Says: our estimate of ②, computed from sample data. ŷ (“y-hat”) is the predicted y at a given x.

For Bridger: from the data (Stop 3): ŷ = 3,165.89 + 257.72x. This is the solid line. It is the one we can actually use.

b0, b1, ŷ computed

The hat on ŷ always means “estimated by us.” No hat on y means a real, observed value. Greek letters (β, ε) belong to the population; Latin letters (b, e) are what we compute from the sample.

Which line is the “best” line?

Your line always passes through (x, y) = (40, $6,902.60), like the least squares line. The red segments are the errors y − y. Make their squared total as small as you can.

The least squares criterion

min Σ(yi − yi)²

Choose b0 and b1 to make the sum of squared errors as small as possible. Calculus gives the answer in closed form:

b1 = Σ(x − x)(y − y) / Σ(x − x)² (14.6)
b0 = y − b1x (14.7)

Customer #xyx − xy − y(x − x)(y − y)(x − x)²
Step by step
  1. Means. x = 200 / 5 = 40 years; y = 34,513 / 5 = $6,902.60.
  2. Deviations. Subtract the means from each customer (columns 4 and 5). A 20-year-old is 20 years below average and $4,540.60 below average in charges.
  3. Multiply and add. Σ(x − x)(y − y) = 267,000. Every product is positive or zero (the 40-year-old is exactly average in age): customers below average in age are also below average in charges, and vice versa. That is what a positive slope looks like in numbers.
  4. Square and add. Σ(x − x)² = 1,000: how spread out the ages are.
  5. Slope. b1 = 267,000 / 1,000 = 267.00.
  6. Intercept. b0 = 6,902.6 − 267 × 40 = 6,902.6 − 10,680 = −3,777.40.

y = −3,777.40 + 267.00x

For these 5 customers, each additional year of age adds about $267 to annual charges, so ten years adds about $2,670. Excel's =SLOPE(C10:C14,B10:B14) returns the same 267.

  • The line passes through the point of means: −3,777.4 + 267 × 40 = 6,902.6 = y. The least squares line always does.
  • The residuals add to 0: the line runs through the middle of the points, some above, some below.
  • Squared and added, the residuals give SSE = 3,380,475, the smallest value the slider can reach. No other line does better.
  • The first two column sums are 0 because deviations from a mean always cancel. That's why we multiply them before adding.
  • The intercept, −$3,777, is negative. Hold that thought until Stop 5.
Why least squares? Why not some other rule?

Every line misses some customers. To pick the “best” line we need one number that scores how badly a line misses, then choose the line with the best score. There are three obvious candidates. Here they are for the same 5 customers, on six lines that all pass through the point of means (40, $6,902.60):

① The plain sum fails. Misses above the line (+) and below it (−) cancel out. For every line through the point of means the sum is exactly 0, the terrible flat line included. It can't tell a good line from a bad one.

② Absolute values don't pick one line. Nothing cancels now, but every slope from $264.80 to $275.20 ties at 3,137.6. The rule can't say which line is best, and there is no simple formula: you have to search by trial and error.

③ Squares give exactly one best line, $267, with an exact formula you can do by hand (14.6, 14.7) or with =SLOPE. Move the slider above: the SSE climbs on both sides of 267.

Three more reasons it's the standard
  • Big misses count extra. Squaring makes a $2,000 miss count 4 times as much as a $1,000 miss, not 2. For an insurer, one badly mispriced customer hurts more than several small misses, so this is a sensible priority. The flip side: a few very large charges (Bridger's smokers) pull the line toward them. Residual analysis (14.8) checks for that.
  • It's the same idea as the mean. Of all single numbers, y is the one that makes Σ(y − c)² smallest. Least squares extends that to a line, which is why the line always passes through (x, y). The flat “just predict the mean” line in the table has SSE = 74,669,475 = SST.
  • Everything else in Chapter 14 is built on it. SST = SSR + SSE and r² (14.3), and the t test and intervals (14.5–14.6), only work this neatly for the least squares line.

For Bridger, in one sentence: least squares picks the age-based pricing line whose squared pricing errors, added up over all customers, are as small as possible.

STOP 5 · 14.2 · READING THE INTERCEPT

Does a newborn cost Bridger −$2,091?

Non-smoker line: y = −2,091.42 + 267.25x. Plug in x = 0 and you get −$2,091, a customer who pays Bridger to be insured.

Predict first

What's wrong?

The youngest customer is 18 and the oldest is 64. The line describes customers aged 18 to 64. At x = 0 it is extrapolating: predicting far outside the data, where nothing says the straight line still holds.

  • Excel made no error. Least squares only tries to fit the customers it has, aged 18 to 64. It never had to make sense at age 0.
  • Inside the data, the line is fine. At 18 it predicts −2,091.42 + 267.25 × 18 = $2,719, a sensible positive number. The line only drops below $0 before age 7.8, where there are no customers.
  • The sign isn't the test. The all-customer intercept, $3,166, is positive, and it is just as meaningless: no customer is 0 years old. A positive b0 can be nonsense too. The test is whether x = 0 is in your data.
  • “Babies really are cheaper” may even be true, but this data can't tell you. There are no babies in it.
Rule for your project

Interpret b0 only if x = 0 is a real, observed value (0 children, 0 nights, $0 discount). Otherwise, say “the intercept has no practical meaning here; it just positions the line.”

The same warning applies to predictions: a line fit to ages 18 to 64 should not price an 80-year-old.

What b0 is: the value of ŷ when x = 0. It is a prediction, like any other point on the line. A prediction is only trustworthy where the line has data to learn from. So the real question is always: is x = 0 inside my data?

Two questions before you write a word about b0
  1. Can x really be 0? Is there such a thing as a customer, listing or order with x = 0? A studio has 0 bedrooms; a full-price order has a 0% discount. No one is 0 years old in an adult insurance pool; no car has a 0-liter engine.
  2. Is x = 0 actually in your data, with real rows at or near it? One or two odd rows don't count, especially if they look like data errors. Check with =COUNTIF(x_range,0) and =MIN(x_range).

Both yes: interpret it, in y's units. “b0 = $___ is the predicted nightly price of a studio (0 bedrooms).”

Either no: don't interpret it. Write: “The intercept has no practical meaning here, because no customer is 0 years old (our data cover ages 18 to 64); it only positions the line.”

Either way, keep b0 in your equation. Every prediction ŷ = b0 + b1x needs it, even when it means nothing on its own.

Where the line has data

Predicting inside the shaded range is interpolation: the line has customers on both sides. Predicting outside it is extrapolation: the line is guessing. The intercept is just the most extreme case of extrapolation when 0 is far from the data.

The same warning for predictions: an 80-year-old
All-customer line at age 80: 3,165.89 + 257.72 × 80$23,784
Customers in the data older than 640

Excel will happily print $23,784. Nothing in the data says whether charges keep climbing $258 a year after 64, speed up, or level off. The line was never shown an 80-year-old.

The line is already shaky at the edge. The 22 customers aged exactly 64 averaged $23,276, but the line predicts $19,660 at 64, about $3,600 low. A straight line drawn through the middle of the data is least reliable at its ends, and beyond them it has no support at all.

Rule: predict only for x between the smallest and largest x in your data. If the business needs a number outside that range, say plainly that it is an extrapolation and should not be relied on.

STOP 6 · 14.3 · COEFFICIENT OF DETERMINATION

The slope says how much. r² says how well.

Workbook sheets: 14.3 By Hand (5), 14.3 Project 1 Contrast, 14.3 Compare.

Split the variation in charges into two parts
Sum of squaresFormulaMeasures
SST, totalΣ(y − y)²How much charges vary at all
SSR, regressionΣ(y − y)²The part the line explains
SSE, errorΣ(y − y)²The part it misses

SST = SSR + SSE r² = SSR / SST

r² runs from 0 (the line explains nothing) to 1 (every point on the line). The correlation is r = (sign of b1)√r².
