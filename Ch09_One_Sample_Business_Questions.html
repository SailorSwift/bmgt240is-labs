STOP 1 · THE RULE

A one-sample test needs three things

Every test in Chapter 9 compares one group to one fixed number. That test is only worth running when your business actually relies on that number.

1

A number your business relies on

A price, a cost, a rate, a promise. Not a number picked because it is round.

2

A reason it relies on it

A budget was built on it, an ad promises it, a policy kicks in at it, or a goal is set at it.

3

A decision that changes with the answer

If the test comes out one way you do one thing; the other way, something else.

If you cannot name the decision, you do not have a business question yet. You have a calculation.

STOP 2 · WHERE THE TARGET NUMBER COMES FROM

Real targets come from four places

The number after μ0 or p0 in your hypotheses has to come from somewhere in the business. These are the four places it usually comes from.

Source 1
A claim or promise

Something the business says out loud: an ad, a guarantee, a contract.

“Our cars cost under $3,500 a year to fuel.”

Source 2
A planning assumption

A number a budget, price, or schedule was built on.

“We priced plans assuming $12,000 per customer.”

Source 3
A policy trigger

A level that sets off an action when it is crossed.

“If cancellations go above 30%, we require deposits.”

Source 4
A goal

A target the business is trying to reach.

“Our profit margin target is 10%.”

If your target does not come from one of these, the test answers a question nobody asked.

STOP 3 · WORKED EXAMPLE · OUR CLASS PROJECT

Bridger Health Insurance: two numbers our prices were built on

From our class proposal: this year's prices assume the average customer costs $12,000 a year and 15% of customers smoke.

The mean · 9.4 (t test)

Our business relies on an average cost of $12,000 because every plan was priced on it; if the test shows the true average is higher, we will raise next year's premiums.

Source 2: a planning assumption. Variable: charges.

The proportion · 9.5 (z test)

Our business relies on 15% of customers smoking because the smoker surcharge was set for that mix; if the test shows more than 15% smoke, we will raise the surcharge.

Source 2: a planning assumption. Variable: smoker (yes / no).

Same dataset, two versions of the question
Just a calculation

“Is the average BMI different from 28?”

Why 28? Nothing in the business depends on it, and no decision changes with the answer.

A business question

“Is the average charge different from the $12,000 our prices were built on?”

The number has a source, and the answer decides whether we reprice.

Before you call it done
  • The target number comes from one of the four sources, and you can say which.
  • You named the variable in your dataset, and whether it is a mean (9.4) or a yes/no share (9.5).
  • You decided how big a difference would matter to the business. With thousands of rows, almost any difference is “significant” (9.6).
