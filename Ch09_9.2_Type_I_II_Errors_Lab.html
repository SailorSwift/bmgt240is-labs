STOP 1 · THE KEY TERMS

A test can be wrong in two different ways

The decision is based on a sample, so the conclusion can be wrong. Recall: H0 is tentatively assumed true; Ha is concluded to be true if H0 is rejected. Exactly one of them is true.

Type I error

The error of rejecting H0 when it is true.

We conclude Ha, but H0 was right. A “false alarm.”

Textbook Ch 9 key terms.

Type II error

The error of accepting H0 when it is false.

Ha was right, but the sample did not give enough evidence, so we did not reject H0. A “missed discovery.”

Textbook Ch 9 key terms.

Level of significance · α

The probability of making a Type I error when the null hypothesis is true as an equality.

The person running the test chooses α before sampling. Common choices are 0.05 and 0.01. A costly Type I error calls for a small α.

Textbook Ch 9 key terms; Section 9.2.

Significance test

A test that controls only the Type I error.

By choosing α we control the chance of a Type I error. The chance of a Type II error is usually not determined. Most tests in this course are significance tests.

Section 9.2.

The jury trial again

H0: innocent  ·  Ha: guilty

Type I error: convicting an innocent person (rejecting a true H0).
Type II error: letting a guilty person go free (not rejecting a false H0).

“Beyond a reasonable doubt” keeps the chance of convicting an innocent person small, which is what a small α does.

STOP 3 · LEVEL OF SIGNIFICANCE

A super simple test: is this coin rigged?

You suspect a coin lands heads too often. You flip it 20 times. If you get at least the cutoff number of heads, you call the coin rigged.

H0: the coin is fair (heads 50%)
Ha: the coin is rigged toward heads

Even a fair coin sometimes gives a lot of heads, and even a rigged coin sometimes gives few. So the decision can be wrong in two ways:

Type I error: the coin is fair, but you call it rigged (a false alarm).
Type II error: the coin is rigged, but you call it fair (a miss).

Level of significance (α): the probability of making a Type I error when the null hypothesis is true as an equality.

Here: α = the chance that a fair coin gives at least the cutoff number of heads. You pick the cutoff, so you choose α.

How to read this picture
1 · RED BARS

A fair coin, flipped 20 times, over and over. Each bar is how often you would get that many heads. Most runs give about 10.

2 · BLACK LINE

Your cutoff. Heads at or past this line → you call the coin rigged (reject H0).

3 · DARK RED BARS = α

A fair coin that, by luck, lands past the cutoff. You would call a fair coin rigged: a Type I error. Add up these bars and you get α.

4 · BLUE BARS

A rigged coin, flipped 20 times, over and over. Most runs give about 15 heads.

5 · DARK BLUE BARS = β

A rigged coin that lands below the cutoff. You would call it fair: a Type II error. Its size depends on how rigged the coin really is, which you don't know. That is why β is usually not controlled.

Slide the cutoff to 18: a fair coin almost never gets there (tiny α), but you now miss most rigged coins (big β). Slide it to 12: you catch rigged coins, but call many fair coins rigged. A stricter cutoff means a smaller α and a larger β. The beer test in Stop 6 works the same way, with x̄ in place of the number of heads.

STOP 4

Why we say “do not reject H₀,” not “accept H₀”

Most tests control only the Type I error, by choosing α. The chance of a Type II error is usually not determined.

So if the sample does not reject H0, we cannot say how confident we are that H0 is true. “Accept H0” would put us at risk of an uncontrolled Type II error.

Only two conclusions: reject H0, or do not reject H0. “Do not reject” means withhold judgment and action.

Note and Comment · the FDA

The FDA must approve a new drug or not. Approving a drug that is not safe and effective is one error; failing to approve one that is safe and effective is the other. Whatever it decides, the risk of a costly error cannot be eliminated.

Approving an unsafe drug rejects a true H0: a Type I error. Failing to approve a good drug does not reject a false H0: a Type II error.
