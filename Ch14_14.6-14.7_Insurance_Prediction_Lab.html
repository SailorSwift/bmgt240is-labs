The confidence band is an hourglass (click Zoom in on the line to see it): narrowest at the average age (39.2) and wider toward 18 and 64, because the line is pinned down best where most of the data are. The prediction band barely changes: one person's own variation dominates everywhere.

The formulas (Excel versions in the workbook)

CI: ŷ ± tα/2 · s · √( 1/n + (x₀ − x̄)² / Σ(x − x̄)² )
PI: ŷ ± tα/2 · s · √( 1 + 1/n + (x₀ − x̄)² / Σ(x − x̄)² )

tα/2 = T.INV.2T(0.05, n−2) = 1.9617 · s = STEYX · x̄ = AVERAGE · Σ(x − x̄)² = DEVSQ · ŷ = FORECAST.LINEAR(x₀, y, x)

STOP 4 · 14.6 · WHY SO DIFFERENT?

One extra “1” inside the square root

Both intervals start from the same ŷ. The difference is what they have to cover:

Confidence interval: only our uncertainty about where the line is. With 1,338 customers, that's small.

Prediction interval: the line's uncertainty plus one person's own variation, which is a whole s, about $11,560. That's the extra “1”: 1 × s² added inside the root.

More data shrinks the confidence interval toward 0. It never shrinks the prediction interval below about ±2s, because individual customers will always differ.

spred = √(s² + sŷ²) = √(11,560.31² + 316.54²). The line's uncertainty is a sliver: almost all of the width comes from the person.

STOP 5 · 14.6 · REALITY CHECK

Does the prediction interval really catch 95% of customers?

Workbook sheet: 2 Predict, steps 5–6.

The 40-year-olds. Bridger has 27 customers aged 40. The prediction interval at 40 runs from −$9,212 to $36,162. 25 of 27 fall inside, close to the promised 95%.

Everyone. Checking all 1,338 customers against the prediction band at their own age: 1,191 inside (89.0%), 147 above, 0 below. A bell-shaped error would miss about 2.5% on each side. Bridger's misses are all on top: smokers who cost far more than the band allows.

The negative limit. The band's lower edge is below $0 at every age (at 40: −$9,212), but nobody's charges are negative. A “±” interval assumes misses are balanced like a bell (assumption 4). Bridger's aren't: the band is too long on the bottom and too short on top. The interval's width is about right; its shape isn't.

STOP 6 · YOUR PROJECT

Put it in your report

Workbook sheet: 3 Your Project.

Four sentences for your regression section
  1. Significant? “The slope is significant (p = …), so … is related to ….”
  2. How big? “Each extra … adds between … and … (95% CI for β₁).”
  3. Residuals OK? “The residual plot shows … (an even band / a curve / a funnel / lopsided misses).”
  4. One prediction. “At x = …, the average is … (CI …); a single case could be … (PI …).”
Common mistakes
  • Using the confidence interval for one customer, store or listing. One case needs the prediction interval.
  • Predicting outside the range of your data. That's extrapolation.
  • Reading Multiple R as r. It has no sign: take the sign from the slope.
  • Forgetting to tick Labels when your ranges include the header row.
Key terms · §14.6–14.7
Point estimate ŷ = b₀ + b₁x₀
The line's single best guess at x₀. Excel: =FORECAST.LINEAR(x₀, y, x).
Confidence interval
The interval estimate of the mean value of y for a given value of x.
Prediction interval
The interval estimate of an individual value of y for a given value of x.
Standard error of ŷ sŷ
s·√(1/n + (x₀ − x̄)²/Σ(x − x̄)²). Smallest at x₀ = x̄.
Prediction standard error spred
s·√(1 + 1/n + (x₀ − x̄)²/Σ(x − x̄)²). The extra 1 is one individual's variation.
