STOP 1 · WHAT CHANGES WHEN σ IS UNKNOWN

No σ? Use the sample's s, and switch from z to t

In 9.3 we knew σ from past data (Hilltop's cans, MaxFlight's balls). Usually we don't. A new product or a new survey has no history, so we have to estimate the spread from the sample itself.

The one change in the formula

Replace σ with the sample standard deviation s. Everything else stays the same.

t =x − μ0s / √n

Like z, t counts how many standard errors the sample mean is from μ0. It is called t because it follows the t distribution, not the standard normal.

Why t has thicker tails, in plain English

s is itself an estimate: a different sample would give a different s. That extra uncertainty makes extreme t values a bit more common than extreme z values, so the t curve is lower in the middle and thicker in the tails.

Result: a sample has to land a little farther out before t calls it rare.

Degrees of freedom: df = n − 1

df tells you which t curve to use, and which row of the t table to read. Bigger samples give a better s, so the t curve gets closer to z. With 60 travelers, df = 59 and t is almost z.

STOP 2 · THE BOOK'S EXAMPLE · UPPER TAIL

Is Heathrow a “superior service” airport?

A business travel magazine rates transatlantic airports on a scale from 0 to 10. An airport earns the label superior service only if the mean rating from all business travelers, μ, is greater than 7.

The magazine surveyed 60 business travelers at London's Heathrow Airport. There is no past data on how much ratings vary, so σ is unknown and the sample's s stands in for it.

The question: the sample averages 7.25, which is above 7. Is that enough evidence that the mean of all travelers is above 7, or could 60 people give 7.25 just by luck?

What we know
Cutoff for “superior”7
Sample size, n60 travelers
Sample mean, x7.25
Sample std. dev., s1.052
Level of significance, α0.05
Population std. dev., σunknown

Data: the textbook's AirRating file.

Step 1 · State the hypotheses

H0: μ ≤ 7

Heathrow's mean rating is 7 or less. It does not earn the label.

Ha: μ > 7

Heathrow's mean rating is above 7. It is a superior service airport.

The magazine wants evidence before it hands out the label, so “superior” goes in Ha. H0 holds the equal sign. Because only a high rating counts as evidence, this is an upper tail test.

Step 2 · Level of significance in plain English

α = 0.05 means: if Heathrow is really not superior, there's at most a 5% chance (1 in 20) that the test wrongly awards it the label. That mistake is a Type I error. (“At most” because H0 covers every mean up to 7. The risk is exactly 5% when μ = 7 and smaller below that.)

Step 3 · Compute t and the degrees of freedom

s/√n = 1.052/√60 = 0.1358 (one standard error)

t =7.25 − 70.1358= 1.84

df = n − 1 = 60 − 1 = 59

The sample mean sits 1.84 standard errors above the cutoff of 7.

Step 4 · The p-value in plain English

If Heathrow's true mean rating is exactly 7, what's the chance that 60 travelers would average 7.25 or higher?

Upper tail test, so the p-value is the area to the right of t = 1.84 on the t curve with 59 df.

t table: between .025 and .05
Excel: p-value = .0354

That is about 3.5 samples in every 100. We did get such a sample, so either we hit a 3.5-in-100 stroke of luck, or Heathrow's mean really is above 7. Luck is the harder story to believe.

Step 5 · Decide

.0354 ≤ .05 → reject H0

In words: the evidence supports labeling Heathrow a superior service airport.

The t table alone was enough here: “between .025 and .05” is already ≤ .05, so we can reject without knowing the exact value.

Same decision with the critical value

The t value with 0.05 in the upper tail and 59 df is t.05 = 1.671. Reject H0 if t ≥ 1.671. Our t = 1.84 is beyond it, so reject.

Sample mean x 7.25

Keeps n = 60 and s = 1.052 fixed.

t = (x̄ − 7) / 0.13581.84
t table range (59 df).025 to .05
Exact p-value (Excel)0.035
Compare with α = 0.050.035 ≤ 0.05
Reject H₀

The shaded area is the p-value; the dashed line is the critical value 1.671. Drag x̄ down toward 7 and watch the area grow until it passes α.

STOP 3 · READING THE t TABLE

The t table gives a range for the p-value, not an exact number

The t table lists only a few tail areas for each df, so most t values fall between two columns. That is good enough when the whole range is on one side of α.

Four steps
  1. Find the row for your degrees of freedom, df = n − 1.
  2. Find where |t| falls between two values in that row. The table lists only positive t; the curve is symmetric, so use the size of t and ignore the sign.
  3. Read the two tail areas above them. That is the range for a one-tailed p-value.
  4. Two-tailed test? Double both ends of the range.

The largest the p-value could be is .05, so p ≤ α = .05: reject H0.

STOP 4 · THE BOOK'S EXAMPLE · TWO-TAILED

Holiday Toys: will retailers order about 40 units?

Holiday Toys sells through more than 1,000 stores. It must decide how many units of this year's big new toy to make before it knows real demand. The marketing director expects stores to order an average of 40 units each.

To check, Holiday asks a sample of 25 retailers how many they plan to order. It's a new product with no history, so σ is unknown and s is used.

Orders too low (μ < 40)Holiday would make too many and be stuck with unsold toys.
Orders too high (μ > 40)Holiday would make too few and miss sales.
What we know
Director's estimate40 units
Sample size, n25 retailers
Sample mean, x37.4
Sample std. dev., s11.79
Level of significance, α0.05

The question: 37.4 is 2.6 units below 40. Is the director's estimate wrong, or is that just the luck of which 25 stores answered?

Data: the textbook's Orders file.

Step 1 · State the hypotheses

H0: μ = 40

The director is right: stores will order 40 units on average. Keep the production plan.

Ha: μ ≠ 40

The average order is not 40, too low or too high. Rethink the production plan.

Either direction is a problem, so Ha uses ≠ and this is a two-tailed test.

Step 2 · Level of significance in plain English

Even if stores really average 40, 25 random stores won't average exactly 40. Now and then luck alone makes the test say “not 40.”

α = 0.05 means: if the true mean order really is 40, the test will wrongly say “rethink the plan” 5% of the time, about 1 survey in 20. That false alarm is a Type I error.

Step 3 · Check the shape, then compute t

With only 25 stores, the t test needs a population that is roughly normal. The analyst drew a histogram of the 25 orders: no strong skew and no extreme outliers, so t with 24 df is fine.

s/√n = 11.79/√25 = 11.79/5 = 2.358

t =37.4 − 402.358= −1.10

df = 25 − 1 = 24

The sample mean sits 1.10 standard errors below 40.

Step 4 · The p-value in plain English

If stores really average 40 units, what's the chance that 25 stores would average at least 2.6 units away from 40, in either direction?

A sample at 42.6 would be just as much evidence against “40” as our 37.4, so the p-value counts both tails:

t table: between .20 and .40
Excel: p-value = 2 × .1406 = .2811

That is about 28 samples in every 100, more than 1 in 4. A gap like ours is common from luck alone, so it is not surprising.

Step 5 · Decide

.2811 > .05 → do not reject H0

In words: there isn't enough evidence that the average order differs from 40, so Holiday keeps planning production around 40 units per store.

This does not prove the mean is exactly 40. The sample just can't rule it out.

Same decision with critical values

Split α: 0.025 in each tail. With 24 df, ±t.025 = ±2.064. Reject H0 if t ≤ −2.064 or t ≥ 2.064. Our t = −1.10 is between them, so do not reject.

Sample mean x 37.4

Keeps n = 25 and s = 11.79 fixed.

t = (x̄ − 40) / 2.358−1.10
t table range (24 df).20 to .40
Exact p-value (Excel)0.281
Compare with α = 0.050.281 > 0.05
Do not reject H₀

Both shaded tails together are the p-value; the dashed lines are ±2.064. Drag x̄ below about 35.1 or above 44.9 to see H0 rejected.

When is the t test safe to use?
  • Population roughly normal: any sample size works; even n < 15 can be fine.
  • Not normal: n ≥ 30 gives good results in most cases.
  • Highly skewed or with outliers: aim for n close to 50.
